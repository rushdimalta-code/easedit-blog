What to Wear to a Job Interview in 2026

What to Wear to a Job Interview in 2026

Interview attire advice often misses the point. The question isn't "what looks professional?" in the abstract — it's "what signals fit with this specific company and role?"

The goal is to look like someone who already works there, but slightly more polished. You're not dressing to impress with formality — you're dressing to remove friction. The hiring manager's attention should be on what you say, not what you're wearing.

Reading the dress code signals

Before you choose anything, gather signals.

The company's own content — LinkedIn photos of the team, the company website, event photos, videos. What are the people who work there wearing? This tells you the actual culture, not the idealised version.

The recruiter — asking directly is fine: "Is there a dress code I should be aware of for the interview?" A recruiter who doesn't answer this question is unusual. Most will tell you plainly.

The industry — broad guidance, not a rule:

| Industry | Typical interview standard |

|---|---|

| Law, banking, consulting, finance | Business formal or business professional |

| Corporate tech, FMCG, healthcare | Business professional or smart business casual |

| Startups, creative agencies, media | Smart casual |

| Engineering, trades, logistics | Smart casual |

In-person interviews

Business formal

Suit (matching jacket and trousers/skirt), formal shirt or blouse, formal shoes. Conservative colours. No novelty accessories.

Appropriate for: law firms, investment banking, management consulting, senior leadership roles across sectors.

Business professional

Blazer or structured jacket, formal trousers or skirt, dress shirt or smart blouse, polished shoes. May not require a full matching suit.

Appropriate for: most corporate environments, mid-to-senior roles in established organisations.

Smart business casual

Clean, well-fitting trousers or chinos (not jeans), a collared shirt or smart blouse, clean leather or suede shoes. Nothing wrinkled. Nothing that looks like you came from somewhere else first.

Appropriate for: most tech companies, smaller organisations, roles where the culture is explicitly casual.

Video interviews

The same tier applies — but with two specific considerations.

What the camera does to your outfit: Very fine patterns (thin stripes, small herringbone, micro-checks) create a moiré effect on camera — a shimmering visual distortion. Avoid these. Solid colours or larger patterns work better on screen.

Background and contrast: A very light background with very light clothing disappears. A very dark background with very dark clothing does the same. Check the full frame before you start, not just your face.

Technical check the night before: Sit in your chair, open your camera application, and look at the whole frame. Is the lighting in front of your face, not behind? Is the background clear? Do your clothes look as intended? Fix problems when you have time, not two minutes before the call.

What to avoid

Regardless of industry or format:

  • Wrinkled or visibly uncared-for clothing
  • Strong perfume or aftershave
  • Distracting jewellery or accessories
  • Clothes that don't fit well (either direction)
  • Anything that requires you to adjust it repeatedly

Background research extends to attire

The 20-minute company research routine from the interview preparation checklist should include a quick scan of the company's team photos. It takes two minutes and removes a genuine variable from the day.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it better to overdress or underdress for an interview?

Overdress slightly. The risk of looking too formal is lower than the risk of looking underprepared. A hiring manager who sees a candidate in a suit at a casual office notes the effort — a candidate who shows up underdressed at a formal firm may not recover the impression.

What about colours?

Neutral colours — navy, charcoal, mid-grey, white, cream — read as competent and professional in most interview contexts. Strong accent colours work in creative industries. Avoid very bright patterns in formal interviews; they can distract from what you're saying.

Do the same rules apply to video interviews?

Mostly — with two additions. Avoid very fine patterns (thin stripes, small checks) which create a moiré effect on camera. And check what you look like on camera before the interview, not during it: the camera may shift colours and contrast in ways you don't expect on a screen.
