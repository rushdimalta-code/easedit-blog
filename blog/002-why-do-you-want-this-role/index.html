Why Do You Want This Role? What Interviewers Are Actually Listening For

Why Do You Want This Role? What Interviewers Are Actually Listening For

You've applied for the job. You've been invited to interview. And then someone asks why you want it — and you suddenly realise you haven't thought that carefully about it.

The question sounds like small talk. It isn't.

What's Really Being Asked

When an interviewer asks why you want this role, they're not looking for a compliment about the company. They're asking whether you actually want to do the work.

Hiring and onboarding a new employee is expensive and slow. Managers want someone who will stay, contribute, and grow — not someone who took this role because it was available. The question is a commitment test.

Madeline Mann, a former head of talent acquisition, puts it bluntly: when two candidates are equally qualified, the offer goes to the one who wants the job more — and it shows.

The Three Areas Worth Covering

Strong answers touch some combination of these, in this order of priority:

1. The role itself. What specifically about the duties, projects, or problems in this role appeals to you? This is the most important element and where most candidates under-deliver. Be specific about the work, not the employer.

2. The team. Only include this if you've actually spoken with someone on the team and something genuine stood out. If you're guessing, leave it out.

3. The company. A specific detail you researched — a product, an initiative, a value the company holds publicly. Not "you're an innovative company" — that says nothing.

You don't need all three. One strong, specific reason for the role is better than three vague ones about the company.

A Framework for Building Your Answer

Before the interview, work through these prompts:

  • What is it about these specific duties that interests you? (Not the job title — the actual work)
  • What in your background makes this the right next step for your career?
  • What did you find when you researched the company that resonated?

Then lead with the role, add the company hook if it's genuinely specific, and close with why this is the right career move for you right now.

What to Avoid

Location and salary. Never mention either as a reason you want the role. Even if they're part of the appeal — leave them out.

Remote work as a selling point. "Millions of jobs are remote" is a direct quote from a talent recruiter explaining why this answer falls flat.

Generic praise. "You're a leading company in your sector" or "you have a great culture" — these could apply to dozens of employers. If a different company's name could slot in without changing anything, the answer isn't specific enough.

Only talking about the company. If you speak for two minutes about the brand but never explain why you want to do this particular work, the interviewer hears ambivalence about the role itself.

A Strong Answer Sounds Like This

"The reason I'm drawn to this role is I see an opportunity to use my background in logistics optimisation at exactly the moment this industry is shifting. Specifically, I'm interested in building out the supplier integration function — my experience is in creating processes where none exist, and from what I understand about the current setup here, that's where I could make an immediate contribution."

Note what it does: leads with the work, not the company. Ties experience to a specific need. Shows research without sounding like a press release.

The Prep That Makes the Difference

Read the job description carefully — not the overview paragraph at the top, but the actual responsibilities. Find two or three that genuinely interest you. Know why.

That's the answer.
