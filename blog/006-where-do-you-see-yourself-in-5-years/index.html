Where Do You See Yourself in 5 Years? The Two Wrong Answers and What to Say Instead

Where Do You See Yourself in 5 Years? The Two Wrong Answers and What to Say Instead

The two most natural responses to this question are both wrong.

"I don't know where I'll be in 5 years" — honest, but signals indifference. The interviewer hears: this person hasn't thought about where this role fits into their career.

"I'll probably be looking for my next opportunity by then" — also honest, equally damaging. The interviewer hears: this person is already planning to leave.

Why the Question Gets Asked

Hiring and onboarding a new employee costs time and money — often the equivalent of several months' salary once you account for the hiring process, training, and the time it takes for a new hire to reach full productivity.

Managers want someone who will stay, grow, and add more value over time — not someone who will be gone before they've reached their potential. The 5-year question is a commitment test.

The Hidden Trap: The Specific Senior Title

There's a third wrong answer that's less obvious: naming a very specific senior role you plan to be in.

"In 5 years I'd like to be a VP of Engineering" can land badly — it sounds like you're already planning your promotion path, or that you'll be dissatisfied if the trajectory isn't that linear.

Ambition is appropriate. Presuming a specific ladder position at a company you haven't started at yet is overreach.

What a Strong Answer Covers

Three elements, none of which require certainty about the future:

1. Commitment to this company. Say directly that you see yourself still at this company in 5 years. It sounds simple, but most candidates don't actually say it.

2. Growth within the role. More responsibility, expanded expertise, a natural progression into senior work. Describe the direction without locking yourself into a specific title.

3. Learning and development. Mention training, certifications, becoming the go-to expert in your area, mentoring others. This shows you're thinking about long-term contribution, not just the job as it exists today.

The Benchmark Answer

"In five years I'd like to still be working here — ideally having grown in this role into more responsibility, or earned a promotion based on what I've contributed. By that point I'd have completed additional training to broaden my skills, and I'd want to be the person the team naturally turns to for complex problems in this area."

Note what it does: commits to the company, describes growth without naming a title, and adds a learning dimension that shows forward-thinking. It's ambitious but grounded.

Tailoring It

This answer works at a generic level, but it's stronger when you make it specific. If you know the company has a formal development programme, mention that. If there's a technical skill that's clearly central to senior work in this role, say you plan to develop it.

The more it sounds like you've thought about growing at this company specifically, rather than just growing in general, the more credible it is.

What to Avoid

  • "I don't know / it's hard to say" — signals disengagement
  • "I'd like to be running my own business" — signals exit
  • "I'd like to be a [very specific C-suite title]" — can sound presumptuous
  • "I just want to be happy and learning" — too vague to be useful

The question isn't difficult. The answer just needs to signal that you've thought about the long term — and that you see this company in it.
