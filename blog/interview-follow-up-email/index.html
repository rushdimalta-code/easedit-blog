How to Write a Follow-Up Email After an Interview

How to Write a Follow-Up Email After an Interview

Most follow-up emails say: "Thank you so much for meeting with me today. I really enjoyed learning more about the role. I'm very excited about the opportunity."

That email does nothing. The interviewer reads it in 4 seconds, registers nothing specific, and moves on.

A follow-up email that works does one thing: it reminds the interviewer of something specific they heard that interested them. It's not about gratitude — that's expected. It's about putting one more useful data point in front of them before they make a decision.

What to write

The subject line. Keep it simple and recognisable: "Follow-up — [Your Name] — [Role]". Not "Thank you for your time" — that's what 60% of candidates write, and it makes yours harder to find later.

The opening. Reference one specific thing from the interview. Not "I really enjoyed our conversation" — something real. "Your point about [X] stuck with me" or "The project you described around [Y] sounds exactly like the work I've been doing at [company]." One sentence. The interviewer reads this and immediately remembers the conversation differently than they would from a generic email.

The substance. This is the part most candidates skip. Add one thing that reinforces your candidacy — something relevant that didn't come up, a relevant link or example, or a brief articulation of why this specific role interests you based on what you now know about it. Two to three sentences.

The close. Simple. "Looking forward to hearing next steps." Don't say "I'm very excited about the opportunity" — it's filler. Don't apologise for following up — you're not inconveniencing anyone.

A template you can adapt

Subject: Follow-up — [Your name] — [Role]

Hi [Name],

Thank you for meeting with me [today/yesterday]. [One specific thing from the conversation — a project, a challenge they mentioned, something that surprised you.]

[One thing that reinforces your candidacy — something relevant that didn't come up in the interview, or a clearer statement of the fit between your background and what they're looking for.]

Looking forward to hearing next steps.

[Your name]

The total length should be 100–150 words. Not shorter — that reads as perfunctory. Not longer — anything over 200 words is unlikely to be read carefully.

Background research pays off here too

A good follow-up email draws on what you learned during the interview — and what you knew going in. Candidates who researched the company's recent news, the team's current work, and the interviewer's background before the interview have more material to reference in the follow-up. "What you mentioned about [specific initiative] aligns with the work I did at [company]" is only possible if you already knew about that initiative. When not to send a follow-up

If the recruiter has explicitly told you there's a strict process and no contact between stages, don't send one. This is rare, but it happens. Following instructions is itself a data point.

Also: don't follow up more than once before the stated decision date. One well-crafted email is a professional courtesy. Two emails before you've heard back is pressure.

Frequently Asked Questions

How soon should I send a follow-up email after an interview?

Within 24 hours. Same day is fine — and slightly better, because your memory of the conversation is fresh and your email will reference specific things the interviewer said.

What if I forgot to ask something in the interview?

A follow-up email is a natural place to ask it. Keep it brief: "One thing I didn't get the chance to ask — [question]." It shows continued engagement without being pushy.

Should I send a follow-up if I don't think the interview went well?

Yes. A strong follow-up email occasionally recovers a mediocre interview. It rarely hurts. The question is whether you write something specific and genuine — a generic thank-you makes no difference either way.
