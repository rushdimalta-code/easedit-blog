How to Negotiate Salary: A Word-for-Word Script

How to Negotiate Salary: A Word-for-Word Script

Most candidates don't negotiate. Not because they don't want to — but because they don't know what to say, and the silence fills itself with imagined consequences.

The research is clear: candidates who negotiate receive higher starting salaries without meaningful negative consequences in the vast majority of cases. The risk of politely negotiating a job offer is low. The risk of not negotiating compounds over years of salary growth.

This is a word-for-word script. Adapt it to your voice, but use the structure.

Before you start: what you need

  • Your benchmarked market rate (see salary benchmarking guide)
  • The written offer in hand
  • A specific number — not a range

The initial counter-offer call

This happens on a phone call, not by email. Email negotiation is slower and harder to read. Call the hiring manager or recruiter directly.

Opening:

"Thank you for the offer — I'm genuinely excited about the role and the team. I was hoping we could talk briefly about the compensation."

[Wait for them to say yes.]

The ask:

"Based on my research into market rates for this role in [location], and given my background in [specific relevant experience], I was expecting something closer to [specific number]. Is there flexibility to get to [that number]?"

[Then stop. Don't fill the silence. Wait for their response.]

Key principles in that phrasing:

  • You cited a reason (market research, your background) — not just "I want more"
  • You named a specific number — not a range
  • You asked a closed question — "is there flexibility?" — which requires a direct answer
  • You stopped talking

If they say yes immediately

Accept. Don't escalate just because they agreed quickly. A quick yes doesn't mean you left money on the table — it means you asked appropriately.

"That's great — thank you. I'm happy to confirm acceptance at [new number]."

If they say no or push back

"I understand — could you help me understand what the constraints are? Is the base salary fixed, or is there flexibility I'm not seeing?"

This is not a second ask. It's information gathering. Their answer will tell you whether there's room elsewhere (signing bonus, equity, start date, additional leave, early performance review).

If there's a counter below your ask

"I appreciate you coming back to me. Would you be able to get to [your original number]? That's the number I'd be comfortable at."

Hold the number once. If they come back below it again, you have a decision: accept or decline. But hold it once — candidates who immediately drop their number signal they didn't believe it in the first place.

Accepting the final offer

"That works for me. I'd like to accept at [agreed number]. I'll send a written confirmation today."

Then send the written confirmation the same day — see how to accept a job offer for the exact format.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will negotiating make the company rescind my offer?

Rarely. Employers expect negotiation — the vast majority factor in a counter-offer when they make an initial offer. An offer being rescinded after a polite, professional counter is extremely uncommon and typically signals something else was already wrong with the process.

What if they say the salary is fixed?

Ask about non-salary terms: signing bonus, remote flexibility, start date, additional leave, equity or performance review timing. Many companies have more flexibility on these than on base salary. "I understand the base is fixed — is there flexibility elsewhere?" often opens a different conversation.

When is the right moment to negotiate?

After you have a written offer. Not during the interview, not after a verbal offer over the phone before you've seen the numbers in writing. Once you have the letter, you have something concrete to negotiate against.
