Questions to Ask at the End of an Interview — 15 That Actually Work

Questions to Ask at the End of an Interview — 15 That Actually Work

"Do you have any questions for us?"

Most candidates respond with one of three things: "What does the day-to-day role look like?", "What's the culture here?", or "What are the next steps?"

All of those are fine. None of them will help you.

The purpose of asking questions isn't just to get information — it's to signal that you've done the background research and thought seriously about the role. The interviewer's response to your questions is useful, but the fact that you asked thoughtful questions is visible to them even before they answer.

Questions organised by what they signal

Questions that show you researched the company

1. "I noticed you [announced X / launched Y / reorganised around Z] recently — how has that changed the team's priorities?"

2. "The role description mentions [specific responsibility]. Is that a new focus for the team, or has it been part of the mandate for a while?"

3. "I came across [specific article or news item]. How does that fit into where the team is heading?"

These require preparation — you need to know something specific before you can ask. See the night-before preparation checklist for the 20-minute research routine that gives you material to draw on.

Questions about the role itself

4. "What does success look like in the first 90 days?"

5. "What's the biggest challenge the person in this role is likely to face in the first six months?"

6. "What skills or approaches haven't worked well in this role in the past?"

7. "How does this role interact with [specific team or function mentioned in the JD]?"

These questions get more useful answers than "what does the day-to-day look like?" They're specific, they push the interviewer to be honest, and they give you real information for deciding whether to take the job if offered.

Questions about the team

8. "How long have most of the team members been in their current roles?"

9. "Has the team structure changed much in the last year?"

10. "What's the working relationship between this team and [another team relevant to the role]?"

Turnover and restructuring are often visible in these answers. Interviewers don't lie — but they don't always volunteer what the answers mean.

Questions about the interviewer

11. "What's kept you here? What do you still find challenging about the work?"

12. "Has the role evolved from what it looked like when you joined?"

These work best with hiring managers who've been at the company a while. They feel personal — and they are — which makes the conversation more memorable.

Questions about the decision process

13. "Is there anything from my background that you'd want me to address further before we wrap up?"

14. "What's the timeline for the decision, and what does the rest of the process look like?"

15. "Is there anything about the role that candidates often misunderstand going in?"

Question 13 is underused. It directly surfaces any remaining doubts the interviewer has — and gives you the chance to address them before you leave the room.

Questions to avoid

"What's the culture like?" — every company says "collaborative" and "fast-paced." Ask something more specific: "How does the team typically handle disagreements?"

"What does the company do?" — if you're asking this, you haven't prepared.

"When can I expect to be promoted?" — too early in a first interview. Save it for the offer conversation.

"Can you tell me more about the benefits?" — fine in a recruiter call, not with the hiring manager.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it bad to say "I don't have any questions"?

Yes — almost always. Having no questions reads as low engagement. Prepare at least three. If they've been covered during the interview, say: "You actually answered most of what I wanted to ask. The one remaining question is..." then ask it.

How many questions should I ask?

Two to three. More than that can make the interview feel like an interrogation. Ask the ones that matter most to you — not every question on your list.

Can I ask about salary and benefits at this stage?

It depends on who's asking. If it's a recruiter screening call, salary discussion is expected. If it's the hiring manager in a first interview, raise it carefully: "I want to make sure we're aligned on range before we go further — is that a conversation for today?" Avoid leading with compensation questions in early-stage conversations with senior interviewers.
