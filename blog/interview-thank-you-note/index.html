Thank You Note After an Interview: What to Write and When

Thank You Note After an Interview: What to Write and When

A thank-you note and a follow-up email are not the same thing, and conflating them leads to the wrong approach in both cases.

A follow-up email is a professional communication that reinforces your candidacy. A thank-you note is an expression of genuine appreciation — shorter, warmer, less strategic. Both have a place. But sending a thank-you note when a follow-up email is expected (or the reverse) misreads the context.

The distinction is this: a follow-up email adds something to the conversation. A thank-you note closes it gracefully.

When a thank-you note is appropriate

A handwritten note (or a genuinely warm, brief email) makes sense when:

  • The interviewer spent significant time with you — a two-hour process, multiple tours, an in-depth conversation about your background
  • You're applying to a senior role where formality signals respect
  • The interview was for an academic position, a board role, or a position where relationships matter more than speed
  • The interviewer went out of their way — arranged a visit, connected you with a colleague, shared something personal about the company's challenges

In most other cases, a well-written follow-up email is the more appropriate and more effective format. A thank-you note sent where a follow-up email is expected reads as old-fashioned rather than gracious.

What to write

The structure of a thank-you note is simpler than a follow-up email.

Three sentences is enough.

1. Thank them for their time and something specific about the conversation.

2. One sentence that reinforces your genuine interest — based on something you learned, not something generic.

3. A simple close.

Example:

"Thank you for taking the time to speak with me on Thursday — particularly for the context you shared about where the team is heading over the next year. That made the role clearer and, honestly, more interesting. I hope to have the chance to work with you."

That's it. Don't add more. The impulse to elaborate usually weakens the note.

Handwritten vs email

A handwritten note signals effort. The effort is the point — it takes more time than sending an email, and the recipient registers that.

For senior roles or academic positions, a handwritten note mailed within 48 hours is genuinely unusual and therefore noticed. For most corporate roles, this will arrive after a decision has been made — which makes an email the more practical choice.

If you're going to send a handwritten note, write it the same day. Don't wait.

Background research improves what you write

A thank-you note that references something specific — a project they mentioned, a challenge they described, a connection between your background and their current work — reads as genuine rather than formulaic.

That specificity only comes from preparation. Candidates who researched the interviewer's background before the conversation (what they've worked on, what they care about) have material to draw on in a thank-you note. Frequently Asked Questions

Should I send a thank-you note or a follow-up email?

In most professional contexts, a follow-up email is appropriate. A handwritten thank-you note is more suited to senior roles, academic interviews, or situations where the interviewer put exceptional effort into the process. For most job interviews in 2026, a well-written email achieves the same result faster.

What if I interviewed with multiple people?

Send one email to the primary contact (usually the recruiter or hiring manager) and mention the others by name: "Please pass on my thanks to [Name] and [Name] as well." Separate notes to each person are only necessary if you had a substantive conversation with each one individually.

Is a thank-you note expected in every industry?

Not universally. In fast-moving tech environments, a brief follow-up email within 24 hours is standard. In consulting, banking, law, and academia, the thank-you note convention is stronger. When in doubt, send something.
