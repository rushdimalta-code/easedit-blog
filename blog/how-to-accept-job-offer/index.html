How to Accept a Job Offer Professionally

How to Accept a Job Offer Professionally

Most people accept job offers informally — a quick "yes, definitely" on a call, maybe a follow-up text. That works often enough. But accepting an offer well — clearly, professionally, in a way that protects you — involves a few specific steps most candidates skip.

The professional standard is: verbal acceptance on the call, written confirmation the same day. Everything important goes in writing, even if no one asked for it.

The verbal acceptance

When you're ready to accept, say so directly. No hedging, no lengthy qualifications.

"Yes, I'd like to accept. I'm excited to join."

Then confirm the key terms out loud — salary, start date, title — so there's shared verbal understanding before the written confirmation. If anything doesn't match what was in the offer letter, now is the moment to flag it, not after you've signed.

If you've been negotiating and they've moved on salary or start date, confirm the updated figures explicitly: "Just to confirm — we're at [amount] with a start date of [date]?"

The written confirmation

Send this the same day. It doesn't need to be long.

What to include:

  • Statement of acceptance
  • The role and start date you're confirming
  • Any agreed terms that differ from the original offer letter (if negotiated)
  • A simple expression of genuine interest

Template:

Subject: Job Offer Acceptance — [Your name] — [Role]

Hi [Name],

Thank you for sending through the offer. I'm pleased to confirm that I'd like to accept the position of [role] with a start date of [date], at the agreed salary of [amount].

I'm looking forward to joining [company] and getting started.

[Your name]

That's it. Don't add extensive enthusiasm, don't recap your whole background, don't restate the terms they already know. Short and clear is professional.

Declining competing offers

If you're holding other offers, decline them now — the same day you accept.

Don't ghost. Don't delay. The hiring world is smaller than it looks, and a professional decline (even of a company you don't want to work at) takes two minutes and costs nothing.

Simple decline email:

"Thank you for the offer — I've decided to move forward with another opportunity. I appreciate the time the team spent with me and wish you well with the search."

No excessive apology. No detailed explanation unless you want to give one.

Before you accept: the research check

Before accepting any offer — especially one you're excited about — spend 30 minutes confirming you've done the right due diligence.

Have you checked the market rate for this role in this location? (See salary benchmarking.) Have you reviewed the contract terms carefully? (See reviewing your offer letter.) If it's a startup, have you checked the funding stage and runway?

Excitement is a good signal. It shouldn't be the only one.

Handing in your notice

Only hand in your notice after your written acceptance is confirmed and the offer letter is signed.

Verbal offers occasionally evaporate. Accepted written offers almost never do — but the risk exists. Protect yourself by sequencing in the right order: accept in writing, then resign.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to accept a job offer in writing?

Yes — always follow up a verbal acceptance with a written confirmation email, even if the company doesn't ask for one. It creates a record of the agreed terms and protects both sides if anything is later misremembered.

What if I change my mind after accepting?

You can decline after accepting, but do it immediately and directly. The longer you wait, the more damage it causes. A short, honest explanation is better than a vague excuse. Burning bridges at the offer stage is avoidable with the right approach.

Should I tell my current employer before I've confirmed the new offer in writing?

No. Only hand in your notice once you have a written offer you've accepted in writing. Verbal offers occasionally disappear. Written confirmations are significantly more durable.
